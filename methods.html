Methods and rationale

Why this instrument

Organizers routinely claim that their work builds something in people beyond a vote, a signature, or a turnout number: a sense that acting together is possible and worth doing. That claim is largely unmeasured. Existing scales capture adjacent things, including internal and external political efficacy, collective efficacy, sense of community, and group consciousness, but none of them measures the thing organizers describe, and none was designed to move in response to organizing.

The Collective Political Agency scale (CPA) is an attempt at that measure. It is an individually held perception of collective capacity: how the us is experienced from one person's point of view. It is deliberately a state measure, not a trait, because the point is to detect change from campaigns, trainings, wins, and losses.

The constructs

Six components, each defined below. The definitions are the object of this review: an item that does not follow from a definition is either a bad item or a sign that the definition needs work.

Moral-affective orientation
The values and emotions that motivate or inhibit acting with others.
Cognitive-strategic competence
The group's perceived ability to read power and choose effective courses of action.
Relational embeddedness
The degree to which a person is woven into networks of mutual commitment.
Collective capacity
The perceived conjoint capability of the group to organize and carry out collective action.
Institutional responsiveness
The belief that institutions and decision-makers respond to organized collective action.
Racial group consciousness
The perceived political power of one's racial or ethnic group and its bearing on one's own political standing.

The three judgments we ask for

1 · Relevance (to Collective Political Agency as a whole)

Reviewers rate how relevant each item is to the overarching construct, not to any one of the six components. The component-level question is the sorting task, and asking it twice would give the answer away: an item can only be rated for relevance to Relational embeddedness by someone who has already decided that is where it belongs.

Keeping the two judgments separate lets them do different work: relevance asks whether the item belongs in this instrument at all, the sort asks where inside it the item lands.

2 · Clarity (of the wording itself)

Clarity is rated as a property of the item, not as a prediction about anyone's state of mind. The question is whether the wording carries a single plain reading: whether it is unambiguous, asks about one thing rather than two, and is free of jargon, undefined abstraction, and technical vocabulary a respondent outside this framework might not share.

Reviewers are not being asked to certify that respondents comprehend the item. Only respondents can establish that, and cognitive interviews with the intended population are planned for the next round (Willis, 2005). Asking an expert to guess at another person's comprehension would produce a judgment neither they nor we could defend. What an expert panel can judge, and judge well, is the text: double-barreled wording, ambiguous referents, undefined abstractions, and readings we did not intend. Those are features of the item, visible on the page and checkable by anyone. An item that is clear to a reviewer who can nonetheless see a second available reading is exactly what the comment box is for.

3 · Sorting

Which construct does the item read as belonging to? Items are presented without their intended labels, in an order randomized for each reviewer, so that the sort is a real test.

How the ratings are scored

Item-level content validity (I-CVI) is a proportion of experts, not an average of the 1–4 scale. Each relevance rating is dichotomized (3 or 4 counts as relevant, 1 or 2 does not), and I-CVI is the share of the panel in the relevant category.

The familiar .78 cut-off is the point at which that proportion exceeds chance agreement for a panel of six to ten raters (7 of 9 = .78). For a panel of five or fewer the criterion is 1.00, and for larger panels it relaxes toward .70 (Lynn, 1986; Polit & Beck, 2006). The threshold applied will be the one matching this panel's final size, and items falling below it are revised or dropped.

The sorting task

The published benchmarks do not exist for a panel like this one. The distributions reported by Colquitt et al. (2019) are normed on large panels of raters drawn from the general respondent population, so an expert panel of this size has nothing comparable to be scored against.

The sort is therefore scored against criteria internal to the task itself: the proportion of substantive agreement (psa) and the substantive-validity coefficient (csv) of Anderson & Gerbing (1991), both computed from the assignments this panel makes and neither requiring an external norm. Items that draw no consensus, or that are consistently read into a construct other than the intended one, will be revised, reassigned, or dropped.

Our next step is to do a definitional-correspondence study with raters drawn from the instrument's intended respondent population, scorable against the published distributions (Hinkin & Tracey, 1999).

Why the reference window is one month

CPA is specified as a state, not a trait. The instrument is designed to help us test hypotheses about whether and how agency is cultivated in groups who take (or attempt to take) political action together. It should be able to pick up change, if it happens, after an agency-cultivating experience: when someone joins a motivating campaign, or wins or loses in a way that reinforces the collective.

One month is the shortest window that still spans a normal cycle of group activity. Most groups meet at least monthly, so nearly every respondent has real events to report rather than an impression to summarize.

  • A week would put respondents in groups on a monthly rhythm at the floor, and would pick up the ordinary noise of a bad week.
  • Six months or a year would buy stability at the cost of the sensitivity to organizing experience and exposure we are trying to assess, and would push people from recall into general self-characterization.
  • An unanchored agree–disagree format invites a trait reading, which is not the purpose of this instrument.

The trade-off we accept is that a one-month state measure carries more occasion-specific variance than a trait-style scale, and test–retest figures may look lower as a result. Reviewers who would recommend a different window, or an unanchored format, are asked to say so in any comment box.

Content coverage

The closing section of the review asks how completely the item pool samples each construct's content domain (Cronbach, 1990; Nunnally, 1978; Colquitt et al., 2019). This is a question about the adequacy of the set, not the quality of any individual item: a pool can be made entirely of good items and still leave half a definition unmeasured. It is the judgment for which expert raters are specifically recommended.

Racial group consciousness is deliberately carried by a single indicator in this short instrument, so for that construct the question is not coverage but centrality: whether the chosen item represents the construct's most central content and makes sense to include for the purposes of this research design.

What happens next

  • Items below the I-CVI threshold for this panel's size are revised or dropped; items that sort inconsistently are revised, reassigned, or dropped.
  • Cognitive interviews with respondents from the intended population test comprehension directly.
  • A definitional-correspondence study with intended-population raters provides the benchmarked comparison.
  • Pilot administration and dimensionality testing follow, with the results reported back to this panel.
